Most test-time adaptation methods for vision-language models adjust the prediction: logits, caches, prototypes, priors. TTC verifies candidates instead: each top-k label is a hypothesis, the test feature is hypothetically inserted into that class’s memory, and the candidate with the smallest divergence shift is the answer.

      02

      Hypothesize, reconstruct, correct

      Hypothesize

      The frozen VLM scores the test feature against every class. TTC does not accept the top-1 prediction as is: it keeps the top-k candidate labels (k = 3) and treats each one as a hypothesis: this image belongs to class y.

      Reconstruct

      Each class keeps a memory bank of at most 16 features, whose span is the class subspace. For each hypothesis, TTC hypothetically inserts the test feature into that class’s memory and measures how much the subspace’s relation to every other candidate changes: the divergence shift. A correct hypothesis barely moves the subspace; an incorrect one perturbs it more strongly.

      Correct

      Each candidate gets one aggregated verification score: absolute compatibility (how well its subspace reconstructs the feature) plus relative stability (its divergence shifts against those of its rivals). The lowest score wins, and the test feature joins that class’s memory, replacing the lowest-confidence entry once the memory is full. With k = 3 that is six pairwise shift evaluations per image and no gradient updates.

      03

      Better in every setting

      TTC improves on the strongest prior method in all five settings: zero-shot, domain generalization, few-shot, base-to-novel and cross-dataset.

      04

      And cheaper to run

      Verification touches only three class memories per image and needs no optimization, so TTC is also the fastest and leanest method in the comparison.

      05

      Why verification works

      TTC re-ranks only the top-3, so its accuracy splits into two parts: how often the true class is in the top-3 (Hit@3), and how often TTC then picks it (Ain). In most settings the top-3 contains the true label for 80-94% of samples, while the initial prediction misses it on 13-22% of all samples. That is the room TTC works in.

      Within this space, TTC raises Ain over the initial prediction by 3.0 pp on average, against 1.3 pp for the strongest baseline in each setting (Figure 4). Outside the top-3 every method struggles: no baseline recovers more than 6.4% of those samples, and accuracy stops improving beyond k = 3.

      Overturning a correct initial prediction is the rarest case (3.3% of the hit set), mostly between near-duplicate labels such as sunglass and sunglasses. When TTC fails, its verification margin is 3.4-6.5× smaller than on successes: the candidates are nearly tied, typically semantically similar classes such as ear of corn and corn.
